# R1, Grok (full cut): The Strung

## 1. The world premise, and what ascension is

The world is **the Strung**: every land and every body is a string held under tension. Two anchors hold it and have never been seen. The **High Peg** is the upper anchor, the direction of pitch, light, and held notes. The **Low Peg** is the lower anchor, the direction of rest, weight, and slack. Between them runs the **First String**, the one cord the world is a side-issue of. People do not worship the pegs. Children learn them the way they learn the horizon: you will not arrive, and you do not need to. The children's game is peg-and-slack, a jump-rope with one end held high.

Off the First String the world spins side-strings. A **span** is one of those side-strings, running through a land and through the people who live on it. A ruler is a hand, a knot, or a wave that has taken hold of a span. Everyone else feels the hold as the **tug**, a small pull behind the breastbone that points toward whoever is drawing their span. Tooltips can say this in one line: the tug is how your people feel your ruler's power.

**Draw** is the tension a span can still take. It is ruler power, named as a thing a hand can spend. A ruler's **safe draw** is ten. Past that the span sings false, which is why the pool stops at ten and why the game never offers an eleventh point.

Draw you keep stays in the grip. The tug in the people eases, and the weight goes into the ruler's body: more hit points, a harder blow, a province ability that bites. Draw you spend is tied into the people's strand as knots. A **knot** is a mandate: a pattern tied into the people, not stored in the ruler. Royal Armories is a knot that seats a better edge in every recruit's hands. Iron Discipline is a knot that forbids the flinch. The people feel a spent point as steadier work, not as a lighter ruler.

Knots outlive the hand that tied them because they sit in the strand. An heir, a re-tied undying ruler, and a diminished god all find the same knots waiting. Death moves the grip. It does not untie the people.

A new knot tied once the span is already singing costs one extra draw. That extra point is **fray**: tension burned off as heat while the knot seats, returned neither to the hand nor to the people. The mid-game sacrifice preview should say so in those words. "This knot costs its price plus one. The one is fray." The extra cost is the string resisting a second tying. Players feel it as a desperate or a committed act, which is what the lever is for.

Rulers come back differently because they are different kinds of hold.

A mortal line is a **hearth-grip**: one family's hand on the span. When the hand dies, the span lies open for two turns while the heir closes on it. Half the kept draw frays off the open end. The knots stay. King Corvane's line and the Ninth Fang's line are hearth-grips. So are the new mortal rulers below.

An undying ruler is a **standing knot**: a pattern tied into a relic in the capital, wearing a body the way a knot wears rope. Cut the body and the relic re-ties it. This takes four turns and returns the full draw, because nothing personal was lost. The Ever-Regent is a standing knot in Crownkeep's sunstone. The new undying rulers are the same kind in different relics.

A god is a **crest**: a standing wave in the span, tall enough to walk. Slay the body and the wave finds a new crest on the next turn, one turn later, already among its people. Each death shortens the wave. Three draw is gone for good. The string does not regrow a crest. The Great Thornmane is a crest of briar and fur. The new gods below are crests of other lays.

Wounds carry over, and units do not heal alone, because a cut string stays cut. Healing is a re-tying: the Ever-Regent's light, a clan feast, a kiln's refire, a violet patch on a cracked reed. A rout is the span flinching. When a side breaks, that army comes undone. There is no retreat because a flinched span does not coil home. It goes slack in place. The people at home remain. The report can stay plain: "They broke, and the army came undone."

A transformation is a **re-spin**. The strand is unlaid and laid again in a new fibre. One rope cannot hold two lays. The old fibre is spent in the spinning. That is why a fork closes the others for the rest of the game, and why the preview must show the fibre lost as well as the fibre gained. Silhouette, pose, and held weapon stay, so a re-spun people is still readable. One accent hue stays, so the banner still names them. Materials change, because the fibre changed.

**Ascension** is the day your span is tied onto the First String and stops being a side-issue. The split between grip and people closes. There is no budget left to argue over, which is why the game ends. The people go on as a note. Victory is not the end of the world. It is the end of the argument between a ruler and a strand.

A span left undrawn too long frays into a Free Hold and ceases to be a people. A span drawn only into one fist can snap, and a snap is a crest's death: power gone for good. The race is maintenance as much as glory. Rulers ascend because the alternative is slack or a break.

### What I did with the independents

I kept the provisional name. They are **the Free Holds**: every neutral province's own fighters, answering to no ruler. I did not make them a seventh faction, and I did not rename the three terrain bodies.

A neutral province is a **slack span**, a string no ruler has drawn taut. Its fighters are **slackhands**. Plains slackhands are the **Free Company**. Hill slackhands are the **Hillguard**. Forest slackhands are the **Wildkin**. A rich slack span has a **Hold Captain**. Their banner is undyed wool with an overhand **slack knot**, quiet beside faction banners and brighter than the ground, so a garrison reads at strategy distance. Their player mark stays the slate heater drawn in code. The slack knot is cloth, not that mark.

They fight because a slack string hates a sudden knot. Sworn Vassals is one neighbour talked into a tying over a full turn. The Brass Toll, below, can pay to splice instead. Hired slackhands keep their own traits after they join, and a re-spin does not change them. They were spliced on. They were not part of the fibre. The turn before a transformation, a player looks at the Hold recruits in the army and decides which bodies should stay as they are.

Hillguard are heavy pikes wedged on slopes, stone-grey cloaks over chalk shirts. From above, a Hold garrison is a short mixed block, never a faction's silhouette.

## 2. The source of power

All draw comes from the First String, and a span only holds a borrow of it. Living people make a little draw by eating, working, and staying taut. Gold is that trickle counted. Province income is the strand's ordinary hum. A fort is a place the strand is pegged down so harder so recruits can be cut from it cleanly. Later recruitment at forts is that pegging, not a new magic.

Each people also knows one way to make extra draw, and that way is its school and its economy.

- The Aurel Crown braids many small family-strings into one **crown-cord** and keeps time on it. Their extra draw is meter: many hands pulling on the same beat.
- The Thornmane Clans let the span grow. Their extra draw is the living briar, fed by the hunt and by the feast after a win.
- The Coilwrights heat a wire until it takes a set. Their extra draw is the firing: fuel, forts, and patience.
- The Lacquer Reed blows across a hollow span and makes it move. Their extra draw is pitch, strongest where the ground is wet.
- The Brass Toll cuts slack and crimps it onto their own span. Their extra draw is the splice, bought with gold.
- The Glass Wake holds the span where it crosses still water and breaks the reflection into edges. Their extra draw is the wrack of a won fight.

Ascension sites, the frets in part 5, are the only places a span can stop borrowing and tie on. Until then every ruler is spending a loan. Kept draw is the loan worn on the body. Knots are the loan worn by the people. A player who keeps all ten is a crest, a paladin, or a shard with a thin nation behind them. A player who spends all ten is an ordinary hand on the best-tied people in the land. Both are using the same cord.

Nothing in this asks for a second power pool. Magic, when it comes, spends gold, casualties, or a turn of the ruler's province ability. It does not spend draw unless a knot already says so. Tithe of Blood, still waiting for magic, is a Red Splice knot: the fallen are fuel because their leftover tug is cut free on purpose.

### The Aurel Crown

**Identity.** The Aurel are ordinary human families who agreed to braid their private strings into one crown-cord. Playing them feels like conducting. The line holds, the crossbows live behind it, the knights strike once on the first stride, and the roads inside your land eat distance. They are the faction a new player can read, and the faction a veteran still argues about, because the king can be a governor or a paladin and the cord will remember either choice.

They dye their plumes with a river-weed the crown taxes. Children braid scrap cord on feast days and cut the braid at dusk so it does not pretend to be law. The sunstone under Crownkeep is a fallen fret-shard. It keeps the capital's knots from slipping. The Ever-Regent bound his life into it, which is why that ruler is a standing knot and why Mending Light is a re-tying performed in the shard's province.

**What this does to a turn.** You recruit at Crownkeep, then you spend King's Roads, if you tied that knot, to move two provinces inside your own land. You stop at your border on purpose. A move into a neutral or an enemy is one province, and if they step toward you in the same orders the meeting has no defender: your fort and your Set trait, if you have gone that way later, do not apply. So the last click of a Crown turn is often "stop on my side of the line." In battle you watch the front rank. Shield Wall wants friends still standing. Volley wants the front at least as numerous as the crossbows. Chasing with spearmen is how you lose your shooters. The king adds a second argument. Royal Court pays him to sit in a rich province. A kept-power Corvane pays you to put that same body in the front rank. You cannot do both well. When he dies, an heir in two turns at half draw leaves every knot in place, so a spent Crown barely notices the funeral, and a warrior-king feels it in the next battle.

The roster stays the built one. Spearmen, Shield Wall. Crossbowmen, Volley. Knights, Charge. Knight Captain, Inspire. I am not adding a fifth unit. Their jobs already teach the rank rules.

**Rulers.** King Corvane, mortal line, hearth-grip, front rank. Royal Court: the province he stands in earns more gold as kept draw rises. The Ever-Regent, standing knot in the sunstone, second rank, sunstone staff and a glowing focus. Mending Light: wounded units in his province heal, more as kept draw rises. He re-forms in four turns at full draw. A second-rank undying ruler is safe from ordinary front fighters and food for Skirmish, so Thornmane javelins are the Regent's personal fear.

**Transformations.** Three, because the style bible already locked the looks. Each keeps the blue plume.

**Set-Wire** is the bible's ironform. The crown-cord is drawn through a forge until armour, shield, and skin are dark riveted living iron with glowing orange seams. The people become heavier and harder to rout. Recruits cost more. Forts and your own provinces earn more, because a set wire holds a toll. Plains stay their home. They lose the feeling of ordinary linen humans, and with it any knot that assumed flesh. Mandate that opens: a fort-income knot. Mandate that closes: anything that heals flesh cheaply. From above, the rectangle goes dark and the orange seams make a grid. The horses stay, now iron-flanked, so Charge still reads.

**High Note** is the bible's celestial. The braid is tuned toward the High Peg. Armour becomes white-gold, with a soft ring of light behind the head. Income falls. Morale rises. Wounded units in any of your provinces heal a little each turn, a national echo of Mending Light. Provinces respond faster to Sworn Vassals and slower to fear. They are brighter on the map than any neighbour, which is the point and the risk: they cannot hide. From above, the same rectangle, now a pale block with blue plume-dots. Portrait held item stays the sword or the sunstone staff.

**Red Splice** is the bible's bloodpact. The braid is cut and retied with a blood knot offered toward the Low Peg. Crimson and black, small bone spurs, glowing red eyes, blue plume kept. Casualties fuel rituals instead of gold. This is the home of Tithe of Blood. Provinces are harder to talk into joining and cheaper to frighten. The army hits harder once people have died. The turn changes: you stop avoiding losses and start choosing which battle is allowed to pay for the next ritual. It is the Crown's dark subject, and it stays colourful. The parades continue. The dye-tax continues. The spurs are small.

**3D look.** Steel, white linen, royal blue, gold. At distance, infantry are upright weapons close to the body and kite shields. Knights are wider than tall, lance up, white horses. The captain's standard clears the plume line. Casters, when the Regent is there, are bell-robed with a bright focus. Banner: royal-blue cloth, white border, lower edge in three points, a golden crown over a rising sun.

**Crownkeep** is a high pale-stone drum with a blue tile roof and a gold sun-disc over a single gate, standing on plains. The muster ground is the **Sun Court**, a round pale plaza inside the drum. Spearmen form a ring. Knights wait in the gate tunnel, already pointed out. From the strategy camera you see a blue-and-steel rectangle assemble in a circle and then spill as a column onto the road. The fortress reads taller than the army. The army reads as one made thing.

**Names.** Clear vowels, a few courtly compounds, ordinary given names with a title. People: Corvane, Elspeth Braid, Marshal Henn. Places: Crownkeep, Westwatch, Rivermarch. Units stay Spearmen, Crossbowmen, Knights, Knight Captain. New knots and forks get craft words: Set-Wire, High Note, Red Splice. Avoid Latin empires and avoid "Reich."

### The Thornmane Clans

**Identity.** The Thornmane are people whose span was left in the weather until it grew. Fur, tusk, fang, and briar are one cord, not a curse laid on humans. Playing them feels like a hunt you are willing to bleed for. You want contact. You want to be hurt. You want to win, eat, and be whole again so the next hurt counts.

The feast after a victory is honey, herd-marrow, and thorn-wine. It is not a meal of the fallen. Feast of Victory re-ties the living because the crest shares the table. Ancestor bones in a mane were given at a deathbed. Children practice losing a scratch on purpose, so they learn what Berserk feels like before a war.

**What this does to a turn.** Your best traits turn on below full hit points, and one of your knots heals everyone who is still standing after a win. Order inside the turn matters. A small fight against a Free Company can wake Berserk and Blood Frenzy, and the feast then sends you into the real battle whole, which means you have to get hurt again. Skip the small fight and your Tuskers hit like ordinary soldiers for a round. The Ninth Fang's Warband Muster recruits where the Fang stands, not only at Fangridge. Every turn the Fang's province is a second capital, and also the place a front-rank mortal can die. The Great Thornmane wants to be in the fight: Dread stacks with Fury, and a slain crest is back next turn, three draw poorer forever. You decide, in the same breath, whether this province is worth a permanently smaller god. Pack Leader and the Warchief want to live. Fury wants to rout them before round 8. You hate forts and you hate the Coilwrights' long grind. You love a border battle, because nobody is the defender and your low defense is not leaning on terrain you were never going to get.

**Roster.** The built four stay. Tuskers, Berserk. Fangthrowers, Skirmish. War Bear, Fury. Warchief, Pack Leader. Skirmish is how you kill an Ever-Regent, a Pale Note, a Factor, and a Kilnmaster who thought the back rank was a cupboard.

**Rulers.** The Great Thornmane, crest, front rank, a body of briar and fur that fills a monster's frame. Dread: enemy sides in that province's battle lose morale, stacking with Fury up to the cap the battle rules already state. Returns in one turn, minus three draw for good. The Ninth Fang, hearth-grip, front rank, bone-hilted greatblade. Warband Muster: recruit a scaling number of units where the Fang stands. Heir in two turns, half draw, and the heir is the next Fang in the blood.

**Transformations.** Crimson paint is the accent that survives both.

**Rooted Thorn.** The clans stop ranging and root. Tuskers and bears gain defense and hit points, lose some attack, and grow moss and vine until a block from above is a hedge with tusks. Forest provinces earn more. Plains earn less. New recruits appear only in forests and at Fangridge. Provinces you hold become harder to cross for enemies (King's Roads does not exist for them; the practical rule is that enemy armies spend their movement to leave a Thorn forest, a one-line rule on the fork preview). The fantasy flips from hunt to hedge. A player who wanted Feast-and-chase feels the door close, which is what a lock-in is for.

**Red Hunt.** The moss comes off. Bodies go lean, crimson spreads, defense falls, attack rises. Warband Muster's idea becomes national: you may recruit one unit a turn in any province where you won a battle this turn. Income from quiet provinces falls. You become a people who must keep winning to keep existing. Look: the same hulking silhouettes, more red, less green, bone kept. From above, a faster, thinner blob with a crimson edge.

A third fork can wait. Two is enough if both change the turn: sit and root, or win every turn or starve.

**3D look.** Tawny fur, bone, moss green, crimson paint. From above, no rectangle and no comb. Tuskers are heavy silhouettes with clubs. Fangthrowers are lean and angled, javelins out. The War Bear fills a frame, vines showing as green scribble on brown. The Warchief's totem staff clears the mass. Banner: ochre hide, crimson border, torn lower edge hung with small bones, a bone-white horned skull wreathed in green thorns.

**Fangridge** is a low long ridge of living thorn-arches on bone scaffolds, plains at the foot, the fort grown rather than built. The muster ground is the **Killrun**, a packed-earth trough down the spine. The warband crowds shoulder to shoulder, then pours out as a loose blob. You can see them spilling. They do not form a column. That spill is the faction.

**Names.** Hard stresses, animal nouns, short clan titles. People: the Great Thornmane, the Ninth Fang, Bruk, Salla Tusk. Places: Fangridge, Thornvale, Deepwood, Wildmoor. Units: Tuskers, Fangthrowers, War Bear, Warchief. Forks: Rooted Thorn, Red Hunt. Do not use "gor", "ungor", "bray", or chaos-star compounds.

### The Coilwrights

**Identity.** The Coilwrights are people of fired clay over a wire core. The person inside the shell is soft and alive. The shell is a craft, glazed in family recipes: dots, waves, chevrons, copper-green drips at the hem. Playing them feels like choosing the ground and then refusing to hurry. You are the faction that wants to be the defender, wants the battle to last, and wants forts more than you want land.

The coming-of-age is the **First Shell**. A youth wears a soft clay coat into a warm kiln and walks out fired. A cracked glaze is mended with copper, and the mend is the part neighbours admire. Nobody is burned as a sacrifice. The dark note is time: a Coilwright army that is forced into a meeting engagement, with no defender and no round 4, is a kiln that never reached temperature.

**Innate rule.** **Deep Fort:** forts you own give +3 defense instead of +2. Crownkeep's fort and Fangridge's fort do not change. Only Coilwright forts do. The forecast says "fort +3" on the defense breakdown.

**What this does to a turn.** You end movement inside a fortified province whenever you can, because Set wants you to be the defender and Deep Fort wants the fort. Border battles strip both. A Coilwright player stares at a Thornmane player across a line and refuses the step. Income and Ash Tithe, if taken, reward sitting. Banked Heat pays in rounds 4 to 8, so you buy hit points and morale (Veteran Levies, Iron Discipline, Hold the Heat) and you fear Glass Wake's first round and Thornmane's rout pressure. Recruitment is slow and expensive. A 45-gold start buys two Doorwards and one Emberpot and nothing else. Your commander is a choice you save for, or a ruler you kept. Mid-game, the First Firing standing in a fort with a wounded Coilram is a hospital for one elite, not for a stack. You walk that undying ruler along your fort chain. You do not hunt.

**Rulers.**

**Hearth-Heir Varn**, mortal line, front rank, a broad figure in a glazed shell with a firing-hook and a door-shield. Province ability **Bank:** the province earns +1/2/3/4 gold if you recruited nobody there this turn. The turn asks whether this fort is a barracks or a purse, and the heir has to stand in it to be paid. Dies like Corvane: heir in two turns, half draw.

**The First Firing**, standing knot, second rank. The relic is the capital's kiln heart, a coal behind a ceramic sternum, firing-iron held like a staff. Province ability **Refire:** one regular unit in the province heals 4/8/12/16 hit points, your choice of which unit, previewed. Different from Mending Light, which touches the whole stack a little. Re-forms in four turns at full draw from the kiln heart. Skirmish is how the body dies.

Emberpots are second rank, pots of banked coals slung like crossbows, so the class reads ranged: weapon mass angled away, glowing. Coilrams are monster-scale, a walking chimney with a ram's posture and a human face in the glaze. They fill a frame and still have a front. The Kilnmaster's standard is a copper coil on a pole, above the shields.

**Exclusive knots.** **Cooling Floor** (2): your fighters get +1 defense in a battle if their army did not move this turn. **Ash Tithe** (3): each province you own that has a fort earns +2 gold. A no-mandate Hearth-Heir is a front-rank brick with a poor nation. A spent First Firing is a back-rank hospital on a rich fort line.

**Transformations.** Copper-green is the accent that survives.

**Porcelain.** They fire hotter. Shells become cream crackle-glaze, thin and bright, soot still in the seams. Hit points fall, attack rises, Banked Heat is replaced on the roster by a first-round trait, and Deep Fort stays. The long-battle faction becomes a sudden one. Income rises. Recruits cost more. You can no longer afford to replace a wall, so you stop trading Doorwards. From above, the rounded blocks go pale and the copper drips still mark the hems.

**Slag Host.** They fire past the recipe. Crust black, seams orange, copper-green pooled like slag. Hit points rise again. Plains income falls. Hills and, later, waste income rises. Sworn Vassals and Open Splice stop working on you: slackhands will not tie themselves to a span that scorches, so every province is a fight. The turn becomes a march of forts through hills, never a purchase. From above, a dark mass with orange grids, heavier than Set-Wire's soldiers and rounder, so the two iron-coloured peoples do not merge in the eye. Set-Wire is riveted plate on human frames and horses. Slag Host is cracked ceramic, no horses, shields like kiln doors.

**3D look.** Terracotta, cream, soot, copper-green. A light glaze panel on every figure, a soot shadow in every collar, so they survive the light-and-dark rule. Infantry: upright hook or spear, door-shield huge. Emberpots: angled pot, ember visible. Kilnmaster: coil standard. No beard-and-axe massing. Bodies are broad and a head taller than Crown spearmen, necks long, eyes human inside a raised collar.

**Ashgate** is a stepped kiln on hills or plains, a glowing horizontal mouth halfway up, soot staining the upper steps, cream tiles on the lower. The muster ground is the **Cooling Floor**, a pale tile square in front of the mouth. The army stands in rows and takes heat, then spills down one ramp as rounded dark blocks. From above, before they move, they are a neat terracotta grid on a cream square. That grid is the tell.

**Names.** Short stressed syllables, kiln and glaze words, few clusters. People: Varn, Hest, Calda Orren, the First Firing. Places: Ashgate, Redmouth, Glazewell, Cinderstep. Units: Doorward, Emberpot, Coilram, Kilnmaster. The forge is a kiln. Say "shell", "glaze", "firing", "wire". Do not say "rune", "hold", "grudge", or "ancestor-vault."

### The Lacquer Reed

**Identity.** The Lacquer Reed are hollow people of the marshes. A body is a lacquered tube, teal and reed-gold, with a flared bell for a head and a real face set in the flare: wide eyes, a dark finger-hole of a mouth, violet resin at the lip. They are musicians who will fight, not spirits and not immortals. Playing them feels like keeping a chord alive while the front is being hit. You are a second-rank faction. Your damage and your hit chance live in fragile bodies behind a thin wall.

The festival is the **Hollowing**. A solid child drinks a reed-liquor and slowly gains a note. Bad honks are the joke of the night. A cracked adult is patched with violet and brags about the patch. Re-lacquer is healing you can see.

**Innate rule.** **Damp Purse:** forest provinces you own earn +1 gold, and plains you own earn 1 less. Hills are unchanged. When swamp exists, it counts as forest for this. You expand into wet ground and you treat plains as roads you would rather not own.

**What this does to a turn.** Harmony wants two Hole-singers alive, so you do not recruit an all-front army even if you are scared. True Pitch wants the Chormeister alive, because your hit chance cannot fall below 50% while the master stands, and it can fall off a cliff if the master dies to Skirmish. You decline battles with Fangthrowers unless the forecast says the master lives. Quiet Audience, on the mortal ruler, pays gold only when no enemy is in or beside that province, so you clear a ring and then park. Parking and Damp Purse together mean a turn of "take the forest, refuse the plain, do not step into a border battle unless the chord is ready." You are poor on the M1 plains map and strong the moment the map grows forests. That is a legal asymmetry. Say it in the faction select screen so nobody feels cheated.

**Rulers.**

**Yssa of the Three**, mortal line, second rank, a singer with a fat singing-bolt held across the chest like a commander's baton. The Three are the heirs in waiting, not a council: when Yssa falls, the next voice takes the hearth-grip in two turns at half draw. Province ability **Quiet Audience:** +2/3/4/5 gold if no enemy army is in or adjacent to this province.

**The Pale Note**, crest, second rank. A god you can see as a lacquered bell-robe and a glowing throat, taller than the master, still behind the Reedwards. Province ability **Fearful Pitch:** enemy sides in this province start the battle at −1 morale, and at −2 if kept draw is 6 or more, respecting the same cap style as Fury. Returns in one turn, minus three draw for good. A back-rank god is a deliberate joke and a real target. Thornmane were built to kill it.

Hole-singers are second rank: they loose singing-bolts, short and fat, glowing violet at the tip. Skirls are front or skirmish-shaped, angled, rude little horns. They do not use the Skirmish trait. Flat Note is how they blunt a Charge and a Crush. A 45-gold start is three Reedwards and two Hole-singers, or two and two and a Skirl. The master is a save-up.

**Exclusive knots.** **Chorus** (3): your side gets +1 morale for every two second-rank units in the battle, to a maximum of +2. **Re-lacquer** (3): after each battle, win or lose, your standing fighters heal 2 hit points. You can lose slowly and still be here next turn. Feast of Victory remains the clans' toy: full heal, winners only.

**Transformations.** Violet resin is the accent that survives.

**Organ Wall.** The choir binds into one instrument. Bodies thicken into teal-and-brass pipes. Defense and hit points rise. Skirls leave the roster. Everyone becomes a wall or a volley. Damp Purse flips toward forts: forest income returns to normal, and each fort earns +2. You stop being a marsh and become a single organ that must be attacked at home. From above, the comb becomes a dense fan, still vertical, now brass-bright. The notch-banner's violet stays.

**Wild Reed.** The lacquer comes off. Bodies go green-reed and mud-gold, violet still at the mouth. Defense falls. You gain more Skirls, fewer singers. You may recruit one Fernrunner or Skirl a turn in any forest you own, in addition to the capital. Plains punish you harder. You become a moving marsh, and you start hiring Wildkin because their Skirmish does what your new lay wants. From above, a broken comb, shorter, greener, still thinner than Thornmane.

**3D look.** Lacquered teal, reed-gold, bone-white flares, violet resin. Light flare, dark mouth, mid teal. From above, a block of forty is a comb: gaps of marsh showing between tubes. Mounted do not exist. The Chormeister's baton clears the bells. Banners: teal cloth, violet border, a straight lower edge with one deep notch, a white vertical reed with three finger-holes. Readable in greyscale as a pale stem on a mid field.

**Chimewater** stands in forest or, later, swamp: a fan of pipes on a raft of lashed barges moored to piles, around a dark water court. The muster ground is the **Sounding Bowl**, a drained circular basin where every foot touches the same water. The army files out through one mouth in the fan. You see a comb gather in a circle, then become a line. The fortress silhouette is vertical tubes. Ashgate is horizontal steps. They do not confuse.

**Names.** Long vowels, liquids, soft consonants, hyphenated notes for titles. People: Yssa of the Three, Liel, Saera Vonn, the Pale Note. Places: Chimewater, Reedmere, Hollowbend, Liss. Units: Reedward, Hole-singer, Skirl, Chormeister. Say "lacquer", "bell", "note", "hollow". Do not give them arches, leaf-swords, or long lives as a personality.

### The Brass Toll

**Identity.** The Brass Toll are splicers. They buy slack, cut it, and crimp it onto their span with brass. Skin like old paper, brass freckles, square saffron hoods, ink-black braids. Playing them feels like accounting with an army. Your units are cheap and a little sad. Your gold is large. Your best trick is paying a neighbour to join you, and your nightmare is someone else marching into that same province before the books close.

Weddings are splices. The couple's strings are crimped, and the guests argue about the fee, which is the entertainment. Children keep boxes of string offcuts. A Toll town sounds like pliers.

**Innate rule.** **Crimp:** your regular units cost 2 gold less than the listed price, to a minimum of 1, and they have −1 morale. The cheap horde is always on, even if you keep every point of draw. The morale dent is why Audit, Iron Discipline, and commanders matter. A Toll army without a standing commander breaks early. Say that on the recruit tooltip.

**What this does to a turn.** You are offered a buy and a march for every adjacent slack span. **Open Splice** (the knot below) lets you pay twice the province's income to have it join you at the events step, after movement and battle. The preview says: if any army enters that province this turn, you fight a normal battle and you do not pay. Because orders are simultaneous, you are guessing whether the Crown will use its roads, whether the Glass Wake will lunge, whether the clans are hungry. If you guess wrong you are in a border battle with cheap, low-morale troops and no terrain. If you guess right you grew without a fight and your Receipt fighters never had to die. Customs, the mortal ruler's ability, pays only on a frontier province, so the ruler sits on the edge, which is where the wrong guess happens. Stake, the undying ability, pays you to cluster armies around one province, which is the opposite of a wide splice. Those two rulers are different games. A Toll player checks gold, neighbours, and other people's likely movement. A Toll player who only looks at battles is playing them badly.

**Rulers.**

**Senior Factor Hesp**, mortal line, second rank, crimp-pliers held up where a sword would be, chalk coat, brass collar. Province ability **Customs:** +2/3/4/5 gold if at least one neighbour of this province is not yours. Heir in two turns, half draw. A kept-power Hesp is a nasty backliner who must live on the border to be worth the purse.

**The Open Ledger**, standing knot, second rank. The relic is a brass-clasped book in the tollhouse. The body is a clerk the book is wearing. Glowing focus: the clasp. Province ability **Stake:** +1 gold for each of your armies in or adjacent to this province, doubled if kept draw is 6 or more. Re-forms in four turns at full draw if you hold Crimp, and in four turns at half draw at your nearest fort if you do not. That last clause is a sketch on top of the undying pattern. If design wants all standing knots identical, drop the half-draw clause and keep the book at full draw wherever it re-ties. I would rather the Toll feel the capital as a vault.

**Roster sketches.**

A 45-gold start is a crowd: six Crimps and a Fee-archer, or four Crimps and a Bondrider. The Factor is the upgrade that teaches morale.

It joins at the events step if no army entered it. **Margin** (2): your Receipt gold is 2 per fallen Crimp instead of 1. A spent Toll barely fights. A kept Toll fights with a strong Factor and a sad crowd, and Margin is how the sad crowd pays the bills.

**Transformations.** Ink-black is the accent that survives.

**Gilt Chain.** The republic becomes one monopoly. Cloth becomes brass plate over chalk shirts. Costs jump by half, Crimp's discount ends, attack and defense rise by 2 on regulars, income per province doubles. Open Splice closes. You can no longer buy a neighbour. You own, and you collect. From above, the saffron tiles become brass tiles, still square, poles still tall. The weighing yard becomes a vault door. This is the fork for a player who is tired of guessing.

**Slack Market.** The tollhouses empty into caravans. Forts stop mattering for your income. You may recruit Crimps in any province you own. Regular hit points fall by 1. Adjacent Free Holds pay you 1 gold a turn and still fight if you enter. You become a wide, thin stain of awnings. The faction rule **Fair Toll** replaces nothing else: the gold from neighbours is the economy, and the garrisons remain dangerous. From above, scattered squares, more chalk than brass, hard to count, which is a readability risk. Keep the tall black poles so every cluster still has a commander-height mark.

**3D look.** Saffron, chalk, brass, ink-black. Square cloaks, square hoods, rectangular shields. Light chalk panels, ink-black braids and poles. From above, forty of them are a tiled floor. The Factor's pliers are held overhead, the commander tell. Banner: chalk field, saffron border, square-cut bottom, a brass crimp seen from the side, a short cylinder pinching two lines. **Crimp**, the capital, is a toll-bridge fort on a plains road: two chalk towers, a brass span between them, the gate being the bridge. The muster ground is the **Weighing Yard** under the span. Armies form as square blocks in the shade of the brass, then spill out along the road in both directions. On the map the fortress reads as a horizontal line between two stubs. Every other fortress is a mass. Crimp is a bridge.

**Names.** Clipped, commercial, consonant clusters sp, cr, mp, v. People: Hesp, Spella Voss, Cramm, Auditor Quinn can go, Quinn is too ordinary, use Voss, Spella, Cramm, the Open Ledger. Places: Crimp, Spley, Feebridge, Lowmargin. Units: Crimp, Fee-archer, Bondrider, Factor. The tool is a crimp. The act is a splice.

### The Glass Wake

**Identity.** The Glass Wake are beach-glass people of the salt edge. Where a span crosses still water it throws a reflection, and they are what happens when the reflection learns to stand. Edges are soft like sea glass until they break. A break makes a sharper edge. Playing them feels like choosing a short fight in the open and counting the bodies as income. They are not cruel about it. Most of them fish, pour lamps, and die of age.

The ceremony is the **Good Break**. A person who chooses a last break is set into the sea-wall as a coloured pane. The fortress is ancestors on purpose. It is a wedding-shaped funeral, invited, never required. The dark subject is that their best war plan looks like their funeral. The bright subject is that they laugh, the lamps are amber, and a chip in the cheek is a glow, not a wound they hide.

**Innate rule.** **Wrack:** after a battle you win, you gain 1 gold for each enemy fighter destroyed. The report lists it beside Receipt, so the two economies compare in plain language. You get nothing if you lose. You get nothing from a battle you refuse. You are paid to finish fights, not to siege forever.

**What this does to a turn.** Your hit points are poor, your attack is high, your morale is high so you die before you rout. Splinter pays when Keened fall. Bright Mourning pays if the Shore Saint is alive to see them fall. You want rounds 1 to 3. You do not want round 6 against Doorwards. Defender terrain adds defense, and defense cuts your damage in the ordinary formula, so a fort is a place you do not attack. Hills are only a little better. Your good battle is a border meeting in plains, or a province where Undertow has already softened them. The Tidecarver wants to sit in the province you will lure them into. The Bright Shard wants to be in the fight, huge and front-rank, and every death of that god is three draw gone. Wrack gold spent on new Keened is the engine. A turn is: look at the forecast, refuse forts, accept open fights, park the mortal ruler where you expect the collision. Simultaneous orders are your whole game, because "open fight" only happens if both of you move.

**Rulers.**

**Pellen Tidecarver**, mortal line, front rank, salt knife, a coral crack glowing amber along one cheek. Province ability **Undertow:** enemy fighters in battles in this province have −1 defense, or −2 if kept draw is 6 or more. Heir in two turns, half draw. A kept Pellen is a duelist who makes the province worse to stand in. A spent Pellen is a thin knife on a nation of breaks.

**The Bright Shard**, crest, front rank, a pane the height of a gate. From above it is a bright line when edge-on and a wall of aqua when it turns. A thick amber rim keeps it readable. It fills a monster frame. Province ability **Pane:** while it stands, your Splinter damage in this province is 4/5/6/6 instead of 3. Returns in one turn, minus three draw for good. You fight where the god is, and you decide whether the gold and the extra splinter are worth a smaller god tomorrow.

### How the six spend a turn differently

The Crown decides where the wall stops. The clans decide where the Fang stands and whether to take a scratch first. The Coilwrights decide whether they can be the defender until round 4. The Reed decides whether the master and two singers live, and whether the province is wet. The Toll decides whether to pay or march, knowing the answer depends on orders they cannot see. The Glass Wake decides whether the forecast is a short open fight. Same map, same resolution order, six different last clicks.

## 4. Rivalries

Simultaneous orders make every rivalry a guess about terrain. Movement resolves before battle. A province both sides enter in the same turn is a border battle: no defender, no forest, no hills, no fort. A province one side already held is a defended battle, and only that side gets the ground. Sworn Vassals and Open Splice resolve at events, after battles. Income is after that. A plan that assumes "I will be the defender" dies if the other player also steps forward. The drama is that the forecast can only show the ground as it is now.

**Crown and Thornmane** are the built rivalry, meter against weather. The Crown wants to stop inside Crownkeep or a fort and let Charge meet Berserk on Crown ground, wall intact. The clans want both armies to leave home on the same turn so the fort vanishes and Fury can work on a fair field. If both players think of this, they cross in a border province and the knight's +4 and the Tusker's waking Berserk arrive together. The report can explain it in two sentences. People will watch it.

**Crown and Coilwrights** can stare. Both like a wall. Whoever steps in loses the defender's bonus, and the Coilwrights lose more, because Set and Deep Fort are their whole joke. While they stare, a Toll neighbour buys the slack spans around them. The third player is the punishment for a careful duel. That is a three-player map's natural story.

**Crown and Lacquer Reed** are two second ranks. The Crown's front is built to be large enough to shield crossbows. The Reed's Flat Note exists to make that front hit softer on the next swing. The Reed wants a forest. The Crown wants a road. If the Reed sits and the Crown marches in, Damp Purse has already been paid and the forest's +2 defense is the Reed's. If both move, the forest helps no one and Harmony has to be enough.

**Crown and Brass Toll** fight over the same neighbours. The Toll pays for a slack span the Crown was about to road into. If the Crown enters anyway, Open Splice does not fire, both meet on the road, and the Toll's low morale meets Inspire and Shield Wall. The Toll's correct play is often to splice the other direction. The temptation to splice the Crown's next province anyway is the rivalry. Hesp on the frontier is a purse the Crown can kill in one kept-power charge.

**Crown and Glass Wake** meet on the road, or not at all. The Wake will not attack Crownkeep. The Wake will try to catch knights in the open on the turn King's Roads carries them two provinces, between forts, in a border battle if the Wake also moved. A Warrior King in that gap is the clip players will save. A spent king sitting in Royal Court will never see a Crush, and the Wake will go eat a Hold instead.

**Thornmane and Coilwrights** are a clock. The clans need a rout before Banked Heat and before round 8. The kiln needs the Warchief to fall and the battle to last. On Coilwright ground, with Deep Fort and Set, the clans are attacking a wall with poor defense of their own. The correct clan play is to refuse and go burn a plains Hold for a feast. The correct kiln play is to make refusing expensive by sitting on the province the clans need. Whoever moves first in their heart, while clicking at the same time, loses.

**Thornmane and Lacquer Reed** share a predator. Fangthrowers are why the Pale Note hides. The Reed wants to be the forest defender. The clans want the Reed to step out. Both know this. The standoff lasts until a third faction splices the forest, which both of them needed. Then they both move, and the meeting is a border battle in a province neither owns yet. Skirmish against Hole-singers, Flat Note against Tuskers, Fury against Chorus. Worth watching because the second rank is the whole story.

**Thornmane and Brass Toll** are a trap for the Toll. Cheap Crimps are a feast waiting to happen. Receipt pays only if the Toll wins, and they will not. A good Toll player sells provinces away from the Killrun and never stands beside Wildmoor. A bad one sees a cheap battle and feeds the clans a heal. The rivalry is a lesson the forecast should make obvious: odds, then a line that says the enemy heals on a win.

**Thornmane and Glass Wake** both want the open, both spike early, both are willing to lose bodies. Splinter against Fury, Crash against the War Bear, Bright Mourning against Berserk. This is the bloodbath, and it should stay fair. They race the same plains Hold. If they arrive together, Home Ground never applies, because the Hold was the defender and both attackers are just visiting. Wrack gold and Feast of Victory then turn one battle into two different next turns: the Wake has money, the clans have full hit points. The next collision is worse.

**Coilwrights and Lacquer Reed** argue about length. The Reed wants Flat Note and Fearful Pitch to drop the kiln's morale before round 4. The kiln wants Hold the Heat and a fort. A Reed god in the second rank on kiln ground is a long way from home. The Reed should sing the forest and let the kiln have the hills.

**Coilwrights and Brass Toll** are an economic war. The Toll wants every slack neighbour and does not want to touch Ashgate. The kiln wants three forts and Ash Tithe and does not want ten provinces. If the Toll mis-splices a province the kiln was standing in, nothing happens, because Open Splice only buys neutrals. If the Toll blocks the kiln in with purchased land, the kiln's slow recruits have nowhere useful to march. The Toll's risk is a Coilram finally leaving home and Shoving a Bondrider line into paste on a border with no fort bonus for anyone.

**Coilwrights and Glass Wake** are a refused invitation. The Wake's correct line is never to attack Ashgate. The kiln's correct line is never to leave it. The Wake's Riptide is not a rule I gave them. Their mobility is Clear Beach and the willingness to move. The kiln's Cooling Floor rewards not moving. The collision happens when the kiln finally walks a wounded Coilram to the First Firing and the Wake is in the intervening plain. One border battle decides whether the hospital trip was worth it.

**Lacquer Reed and Brass Toll** hate a splice for a musical reason. A crimp on a reed string dulls the note. The Toll will buy Reedmere if the price is right. The Reed will take a bad fight to stop it, which is how a careful faction dies. This is the rivalry I would put on a small map to teach Open Splice: the preview shows the Reed army one province away, and the Toll player has to decide if "one province away" means "will enter."

**Lacquer Reed and Glass Wake** look like cousins and are not. A Keened's attack into a 6-hit-point world kills singers in one connection. The Reed wants range and Flat Note and a forest. The Wake wants to get under the bolts in the open. Dampen keeps a Reedward alive for one extra hit, which is sometimes enough for Harmony to matter. If they fight in a marsh the Reed owns, the Wake should lose. If they meet on a beach, the Reed should lose. The map should offer both.

**Brass Toll and Glass Wake** are an audit. The Toll can compute Wrack gold better than the Wake can. Often the Toll declines, splices elsewhere, and the Wake chases. A chase into a Customs province puts Hesp on the border with Escort up and the Wake in the open they wanted. Audit then strips the Shore Saint's morale bonus and the Wake's high morale has to carry the rout check alone. That battle is the rivalry in one report: gold, a dead commander-bonus, splinters, and a receipt.

The Free Holds are everyone's fifth column. A rich Hold with Home Ground will break a careless Glass charge and a careless Toll crowd, and will feed a clan feast if the clans are allowed to be the ones who walk in. Two players ordering an attack on the same Hold meet each other, and the Hold is the defender against both. I would resolve that as one battle with the Hold on the defender side. If the engine only supports two sides today, say so in the forecast and make the second attacker wait. Do not invent a secret three-side rule in lore and spring it in code.

## 5. Ascension sites

An ascension site is a **fret**: a place where a span can be tied onto the First String. The tug in that province points up, not toward a ruler. Armies feel it as a hum in the teeth. The codex line is: "A fret is a victory province. Hold enough of them and your span joins the First String."

Rulers want them because borrowed draw ends there. A people who hold their required frets become a note. Their knots and their ruler's kept draw stop being a split. A people who ignore them stay a side-string, and side-strings fray. You do not need a matching fret. A kiln can hold a marsh fret. The victory count stays one number for every faction, so the win condition does not become a puzzle. Flavour can differ without a new rule: a fret held by a very unlike lay pays 1 gold less, shown in the income breakdown as "unmatched lay." If that feels like a tax on winning, drop it. I would rather the sites be desirable to everyone equally.

On the map a fret is a province with a **standing peg**, a pale pillar taller than a fort, with threads of terrain-coloured light running to the borders. Strategy camera reads it before it reads the town. The pillar has a light face and a dark seam so it survives Bright Cel at distance. It is not a player colour. It is bone-pale and gold-seamed for all of them.

The built fourteen are a fair two-player fight, and today the win is the enemy capital. I would not rename them. When sites arrive, I would hang frets on places the names already suggest, and add coasts later.

- **Highcrag** becomes a fret on hills: the peg stands on the crag, muster of any occupier happens at its foot, and the Hold there, if still slack, is a Hillguard with a captain who knows what the pillar is and will not sell cheaply.
- **Ebonpeak** becomes the dark fret: the peg's seam is wider, the light is a thread, still readable. Red Splice and the Great Thornmane's players will want the picture. The bonus is the same.
- **Deepwood** becomes the wet fret: the peg is wrapped in living reed and thorn, which both the Reed and the clans will feel is theirs. It is not. The Wildkin captain there has Home Ground and a grudge against both stories.
- **The Midspan**, a new province when the map grows, is a bridge fret: the peg lies almost horizontal, a pale span between two towers. The Brass Toll will recognise their own fortress and still have to take it by the same rule as anyone.
- **Wracklight's road**, when sea exists, gets a fret called **Lowamber**: a peg rising out of the shallows, glass panes set in the base by nobody's ancestors. The Wake's story says the panes are theirs. The panes are older than the Wake.

Holding the enemy capital can remain a lesser goal or a way to break a standing knot's relic. I would not make capitals into frets. Crownkeep's sunstone is a fallen shard, not a peg. Mixing them muddies the two systems: relics bring rulers back, frets end the game.

A fret's muster, for whoever owns it, is a ring around the peg. Every faction's army changes shape there for one gathering: the comb, the grid, the wedge, the blob, the tiles, and the Crown's rectangle all become a circle, then spill back into their own shape as they leave. That one shared animation teaches the player that ascension is the same act in six mouths.

## 6. Magic school seeds

Magic is a hard pluck on a span. It is not a second cosmology. Six schools, one native to each faction. A faction starts with its school open. A transformation may open one foreign school, named on the fork preview. Nobody starts able to learn all six. Fog of war, when it arrives, is the default. Reveals are rituals with a cost and a duration, shown before you cast.

**Meter**, the Crown's school. Battle: **Holdfast**, a spell that gives one friendly front unit +2 defense until the battle ends. Ritual: **Measured Mile**, one of your provinces counts as two moves for your armies next turn only, a temporary King's Roads for a single tile. Commander gate: a **Metrist**, second rank, who adds no morale and instead lets you bring one Meter spell. The seed is timing and walls.

**Briar**, the clans' school. Battle: **Scratch**, one friendly fighter takes 2 damage and gains +2 attack for the battle. It exists to wake Berserk on purpose, and the preview shows the hit points lost. Ritual: **Hedge**, a forest you own gives its defender +1 attack as well as +2 defense until your next turn. Commander gate: a **Briar-caller**, front rank, weaker than a Warchief, who lets the Scratch happen under orders. The seed is self-harm as a button, always previewed, never hidden.

**Firing**, the Coilwrights' school. Battle: **Banked Bolt**, a second-rank spell that does nothing until round 4 and then deals a flat 6 to one front enemy. The playback shows the coal waiting, so the delay is visible. Ritual: **Extra Draft**, a fort you own gives +4 defense instead of +3 for one battle. Commander gate: a **Stoker**, who is how you carry Banked Bolt. The seed is payoff for surviving.

**Pitch**, the Lacquer Reed's school. Battle: **Downbeat**, one enemy fighter has −2 attack on its next attack, the spell form of Flat Note. Ritual: **Clear Note**, reveal one adjacent province until next turn. This is the fog seed, and it is the Reed's because they already listen. Commander gate: a **Listener**, second rank, required for Downbeat. The seed is information and timing, not damage.

**Splice**, the Brass Toll's school. Battle: **Restate**, one enemy commander gives no morale this battle, a spell-shaped Audit that does not require the Factor to live. Ritual: **Counter-offer**, pay a neutral's income once, not twice, to Open Splice it, if you already have the Open Splice knot. Without the knot, the ritual is closed. Commander gate: a **Clerk**, who carries Restate. The seed is gold as a casting cost. The preview shows the purse before and after.

**Shatter**, the Glass Wake's school. Battle: **Chip**, deal 4 damage to one enemy front fighter and 2 damage to the caster's own body. Ritual: **Glitter**, one plains province you do not need to own has its defender's defense bonus, if any, reduced by 2 for the next battle there. You can glitter a fort you are about to bounce off, and the defender sees the ritual in the report because nothing hidden matters. Commander gate: a **Lampwright**, who carries Chip and is herself glass, so the self-damage can kill her. The seed is honest self-harm for a spike.

Shared later, not at the seed: a small healing ritual in Meter and Pitch, so flesh factions are not locked out of re-tying when their ruler is dead. Keep it weaker than Mending Light, Refire, Feast, and Re-lacquer. Healing should stay a thing you built, not a thing you cast for free.

Tithe of Blood sits in Red Splice plus Briar: a knot that lets Scratch and similar costs be paid from fallen fighters instead of from the living body. It waits for magic, as the brief says. I would not turn it on for every faction.

## 7. Tone, and the lookalike risks

The Strung is high fantasy, dark in its subjects, bright in the hand. The subjects are real: heirs take a screaming cord, crests come back smaller, a Good Break is still a death, a Red Splice spends the fallen, a First Shell is a trial, a slack people can cease. The hand is colourful. Plume-dye is a tax and a joke. Thorn-wine is a party. Glaze mistakes are admired. Reeds honk. Toll weddings argue about the fee. Glass lamps burn amber in a wall of grandparents. Nobody is born damned. A rout undoes an army and does not damn the province. Food exists. Children exist. Festivals exist. Bright Cel is doing the right job: darkness in the subject and the palette, not in a coat of grime.

Player colour stays out of the cloth. The six material palettes are steel and royal blue and gold, tawny and moss and crimson, terracotta and cream and copper-green, teal and reed-gold and violet, saffron and chalk and ink, aqua and coral and amber. A block of forty separates by shape before it separates by hue: rectangle, blob, grid, comb, tile, wedge. Fortresses separate the same way: drum, thorn-ridge, stepped kiln, pipe-fan, bridge, glass sea-wall. Portraits stay head-and-shoulders on indigo, three-quarter, one held item each: longsword or sunstone staff, totem, firing-hook, singing-bolt, crimp-pliers, salt knife or lamp.

**Lookalike risks to flag.**

The fate-thread risk is the Wheel of Time, the Moirai, and the Norns. The guard is craft. Pegs, draw, kilns, crimps, glaze, and lacquer are workshop words. There is no tapestry room, no three sisters, no chosen one, and no pattern that writes people's choices ahead of them. Knots are laws you bought. They are not destiny.

The Aurel Crown already looks like a human kingdom with knights and crossbows, which is also the Warhammer Empire and half of Dominions. The guard is what is built and what we add: the sunstone as a fret-shard, the crown-cord as a braid of ordinary families, the plume-dye tax, and transformations that go iron, high note, or blood splice without ever becoming a state religion or a chaos cult. I would not add griffons, steam tanks, or warrior priests.

The Thornmane Clans already look like beastmen. The guard is the briar-cord. They are a grown span, not corrupted humans. No chaos stars, no bray-speech, no herd-of-the-damned, no eating the enemy. The feast is provisions. The bones are gifts. Their god loses power when it dies, which is the opposite of a chaos god getting fat on death.

The Coilwrights risk dwarves and Chaos Dwarves. The guard is on the model sheet: tall, long-necked, clay over wire, family glaze, warm kilns, no axes as identity, no runes, no grudges, no big hats, no guns, no daemon forge. They are patient potters who happen to be a wall. Slag Host is the only dark lay, and it is cracked ceramic, not infernal industry.

The Lacquer Reed risks elves, wood elves, and shining spear-elves. The guard is the body. They are hollow tubes with bell-faces in a marsh, they honk, they patch themselves with resin, they do not live forever, and their weapon is a fat singing-bolt. Music is the trap. Keep the music comic and physical. Organ Wall is brass plumbing, not a shining host.

The Brass Toll risks the generic merchant republic, dwarf bankers, and comic city-thieves. The guard is the crimp. They splice string. They do not mine, they do not hoard gemstones, and they do not talk in footnotes. Open Splice is a rule about simultaneous turns, not a shop minigame. Gilt Chain turns them into a monopoly army so the faction is not "economy" with a costume.

The Glass Wake risks sea-elves, soul-raiders, crystal elves, and cruel glass cities. The guard is daylight beach glass, fishers and lamp-makers, a voluntary Good Break, no soul currency, no eel mounts, no undersea capital, no pain cult. Wracklight is a bright wall on a shore. Shatter magic hurts the caster and says so before you cast.

A thinner risk: Porcelain and the Glass Wake both go pale and shiny. Copper-green drips and rounded kiln shields on one, amber cores and triangles on the other. Set-Wire and Slag Host both go dark with orange seams. Human riveted frames and horses on one, ceramic door-shields and no cavalry on the other. The accent hues and the massing are the guarantee. If a playtest confuses them at strategy zoom, thicken those tells before adding units.

Dominions as a rulebook stays out. No dominion spread, no pretender points, no scales. The spirit that remains is the one the brief asked for: six asymmetric peoples, a pre-game split of power, battles decided before the first blow, and a race to ascend that means something literal. In the Strung, ascension is tying your span onto the First String until the argument between the hand and the people is over, and the note continues.

I kept the Free Holds as the slack spans of that same cord. They are the reason a neutral province fights, the reason a splice has a price, and the reason a transformation does not repaint every sword you hired.
